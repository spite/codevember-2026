Codevember 2026 · Day 14 · Tension

How Tension holds

In orbit, water doesn't fall. It clings to whatever it touches, holds its shape by surface tension alone, and wobbles, ripples and pinches into drops when disturbed. Here is how the coat is traced, how its waves move, how drops tear off and come home, and how the cabin around it lights the scene.

See it live
The idea

Water with nowhere to fall

Day 14's prompt was Tension. Videos from the International Space Station show water doing what it can't on Earth: a film wrapped round a wet cloth, a ball of water wobbling in mid-air, drops pulled out of it on long necks that snap and drift back. Nothing holds it there but surface tension. The piece puts that on a floating object: a coat of water that clings to it, ripples when tapped, rings as a whole, sloshes when the object is pushed, and stretches into tendrils that tear off into drops.

The water is ray traced, not rasterized: every pixel follows its ray in through the surface, refracts, crosses the water, meets the object or leaves again, and sees the cabin around it bent and flipped. The object itself is shaded inside that trace. Everything else (the cabin, its blur, bloom, AgX) is the shared pipeline.

The object and its coat · tools/bake.mjs, water.js objectSDF, coat

A distance field, grown

The shapes are day 3's: Suzanne, a heart, a blob, a sphube, a torus, a knot, a star, an icosahedron and a dodecahedron, each a signed distance field. Day 3 baked them at 48³, fine for its purposes but blocky seen through a lens, so day 14 bakes its own at 96³. The values are stored as 16-bit integers, second-differenced along x and y and split into byte planes, which gzips each shape to about 0.45 MB. The page undoes the differences on load into a float 3D texture.

The water is the same field grown outward: inside wherever the object's distance is less than the coat's thickness at that point. The thickness is never even. A film, plus a smooth pooling field (sums of sine products, so it bends without kinks), plus the simulated wave height below, plus the ripple trains of recent taps. Drops and tendrils are spheres and ellipsoids joined to the coat with a soft minimum, so where they meet they fuse into one surface.

water(p) = smin( object(p) − coat(p), drop₁(p), drop₂(p), … )
coat = film · (1 + pooling · pool(p)) + waves(p) + ripples(p), floored so troughs never bare the object
Tracing it · water.js trace

In, across, and out again

A full-screen pass runs after the pipeline has lit the frame. Each pixel's ray is sphere-traced to the water. At the surface it splits: a Fresnel share reflects the cabin, the rest refracts in with an IOR of about 1.2 (a little under real water's 1.33, which reads better at this scale). Inside, the ray marches at whichever is nearer, the water's surface or the object's. If it reaches the object, the object is shaded there: its print, a GGX highlight from the key, ambient and reflections from the cabin, a soft shadow and AO marched through its own field. If it reaches the surface from inside, it refracts out, or reflects back in when the angle is past total internal reflection, and keeps going.

Most of the work is in the edge cases. A ray that grazes the coat would crawl, so surfaces count as hit within half a pixel's footprint and each step has a floor. A crossing only counts if the ray actually goes through the surface; one that grazes the surface it just crossed and would refract the wrong way keeps marching instead. A ray that bounces too long inside a lobe is let out. Silhouette pixels whose ray passes within a pixel of the water are traced from their closest point and blended by how close they came, which antialiases the edge without extra samples.

Rays through a coat, in cross-section

Rays come from the left. Red ones reach the object, blue ones only pass through water: near the edges the water acts as a lens, which is why the object looks magnified and its outline wavers when the coat moves.

Waves on the coat · waves.js, splat.worker.js

A wave equation on the surface

The coat's height is simulated on the object's own surface mesh, about eight thousand vertices, with the cotangent Laplacian. A pull back to even thickness makes the lowest modes ring, so a tap sets the whole volume wobbling about once a second, the way the ISS's water balls do. Removing the mean height every step conserves the volume. Damping that grows with frequency keeps the waves the mesh can't carry from piling up as noise. Troughs and crests level off softly instead of digging pits or spiking.

The tracer needs the height anywhere in space, not at vertices, so it's splatted into a 64³ texture around the object. Which voxels read which vertices is fixed per shape and is built once by a worker. Each frame the CPU only uploads the heights, about 32 KB, and a GPU pass fills the texture's 64 layers from them.

The mesh is too coarse for the short, sharp rings a tap makes on real water, so taps draw those directly: a ripple train running out from the contact point, a sharp front with rings trailing behind it, thinning as it spreads. The tap also gives the simulation a smaller kick, for the dent and the wobble. Overlapping trains share a steepness budget so they can't fold the surface into chaos, and a thin coat only carries small ones.

A ring of water, tapped

One dimension of the same equation: the dent splits into two waves that meet on the far side. With Restore at zero they just run round; raised, the ring also swings as a whole, which is the wobble.

Sloshing · main.js moveBody, inertialAt

Water that lags behind

Spinning a wet object barely changes its coat: the water just shears along. What makes water slosh is being pushed. The object drifts slowly on a soft spring, never quite still, and a swipe pushes it as well as spinning it. The coat feels that acceleration in the object's own frame, as the simulation's forcing: water piles up on the side trailing the push, swings back past even, and settles. Fast spins add their centrifugal and Euler terms the same way, bulging the coat outward.

Drops · main.js tendrils, sim.js

A neck that thins, then lets go

Dragging off the edge of the water pulls a tendril: a bulb at the pointer, two spheres along a neck, and a foot in the coat, all soft-minned into it. The bulb follows the pointer on a spring, so it lags and overshoots. As it stretches, the neck thins and the coat under the foot is drawn down. Past about a body's radius it snaps. The bulb flies on as a drop, a small satellite breaks from the neck, and the stump recoils into the coat with a kick to the waves.

A drop is a particle with a wobble: a damped spring on its stretch along its acceleration, keeping its volume. Cohesion pulls it home. When it touches the coat it sinks and shrinks over most of a second, and its volume is poured into the coat around the landing point as a swell, which then spreads. window.app.pull(n) pulls n at once, for recordings.

Beads are loose parcels of water riding on the coat (none by default; Beads and Bead size set them). A spring holds each at a rest height over the surface, but the coat can only hold it with so much acceleration (Grip), so a fast spin flings beads off. Free blobs start loose. Once free, a parcel falls back under a weak cohesion pull, overlapping ones push apart, and each wobbles as an ellipsoid stretched along its acceleration, keeping its volume.

The soft minimum, pinching

The coat, two neck spheres and the bulb, joined by a polynomial soft minimum. Nothing tracks the topology: the neck thins as the spheres part and pinches off by itself.

Prints · water.js pattern, noise.js

Patterns with no seams

Finishes wrap these shapes arbitrarily, so a print projected from outside always seams somewhere. Every print here is defined through space instead, and the surface simply cuts it: stripes are planes, spots are spheres on a jittered lattice, latitude bands are cones round the axis, a candy swirl is planes turning with height, giraffe patches are Voronoi cells, coral is a gyroid, wood is rings round a buried log, agate onion shells, Damascus folded layers, marble warped veins. Each edge is filtered over the pixel's footprint.

Their noise comes from a small baked 3D texture rather than a noise function in the shader. That isn't about speed. On Windows, Chrome translates the shader for Direct3D, whose compiler inlines every call and slows down sharply on long shaders. Inlined noise would push the compile toward ten seconds; read from a texture it stays under two.

Changes · main.js updateLook

Morph, sweep, crossfade

Nothing cuts. A new shape morphs: the two distance fields blend over two seconds while the coat swells, the waves fade out and restart on the new mesh, and the size and centre ease across. A new finish sweeps over the object behind a wavering front, each point showing one finish or the other, so the print is still compiled only once. A new background crossfades with its whole lighting. When a change lands, the water is tapped twice to set it moving. Thickness and smoothness ease to new values.

The cabin · backdrop.js, main.js lightFrom

A photo that is also the light

Behind the object is a 360° panorama: an ISS module, a starship bridge, or deep space. It's defocused with a real Gaussian blur on the GPU, widened toward the poles by 1/cos(latitude) so it covers the same angle everywhere and the poles don't pinch. The same photo is the light. A lightly blurred copy, its clipped highlights pushed back up into HDR, goes through the pipeline's environment path for reflections and ambient. The key light points at its brightest region and takes its average colour. The whole cabin turns slowly about a wandering axis, like a camera adrift, and the reflections turn with it.

Sound · sound.js

Hum, drip, slosh

Under everything is the cabin: a spacecraft hum and ventilation, brighter in the module, warmer on the bridge, nearly empty in deep space. Each tap plays a drop and the swell of its spreading ripple. Stirring sloshes in time with the pointer. A stretching tendril squishes, snaps with a bloop and lands with a plop. A push whooshes and swirls, and a morph bubbles. Everything is cut from CC0 recordings on freesound.org, credited in the README, and panned to where it happens.

Using it

Controls

  • Tap the water for ripples, hold for a dent, drag over it to stir.
  • Drag off its edge to pull a drop; swipe to push and spin the object.
  • Drag the background to orbit, freely, over the poles; scroll or pinch to zoom.
  • Shape, Finish, Background and their blur and turn; water thickness, pooling, smoothness, IOR.
  • Spin, Drift, Grip, Tap strength and how often it taps itself (Random taps); Beads, Bead size and Free blobs; Steps, the tracer's march budget.
  • R for a new look, M or the speaker button for sound; it changes by itself every 15 s (Auto change, on by default; Change every 5–60 s), counted once a change has landed.
  • Space pauses, F goes fullscreen, Tab hides the panel, P saves a screenshot (Shift+P at 2×).
