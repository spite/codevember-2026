The photographic pipeline

Every day of Codevember 2026 renders through one custom forward pipeline built on three.js. It is made to look like a product photograph: soft shadows, ambient occlusion and a bounce of indirect light, physical shading, a filmic tonemapper, grain. This page walks through one frame, pass by pass.

One frame

The frame · Pipeline.js

Passes in order

A day calls pipeline.render(scene, camera, time) instead of renderer.render. Each frame the pipeline collects the meshes and their previous poses, then runs: the shadow map, a prepass of normals, velocity and depth, ambient occlusion and indirect light at half resolution, an optional shadow mask, the lit main pass into a multisampled float target, then the optional motion blur, a day's own composite pass and depth of field, then bloom and the final pass that tonemaps to the screen. On the low tier, FXAA replaces MSAA.

Two paths replace the single frame. The capture tier renders 16 sub-frames, each jittered by half a pixel along a Halton (2, 3) sequence, and averages them, which antialiases and smooths every noisy effect. And when depth of field is on and the view holds still, the lens path averages frames rendered from across the aperture (below). There is no TAA on the live path.

The tier is picked from ?quality=, then ?capture, then the day's option; otherwise phones and coarse pointers get medium and everything else high. Every full-screen program compiles in parallel at construction, and compileAsync(scene, camera) compiles the scene's three material variants before the first frame, so nothing stalls on first use.

Materials · PipelineMaterial.js

One material, three variants

Every mesh uses PipelineMaterial. A day customises it with two hooks. vec3 displace(vec3 p, inout vec3 n) moves vertices, and because it runs in every variant, the displacement reaches shadows, AO and velocity too. void surface(inout Surface s) edits the shading inputs per pixel: albedo, alpha, roughness, metalness, normal, emissive, sheen colour and roughness, occlusion, thin-sheet transmission, and a specular scale on the dielectric F0 (1 = 0.04).

The material has a colour variant and two hidden ones, prepass and shadow, that share its uniforms and swap a define. In the prepass, displace runs twice: once for now, and once for the previous pose, with pipelinePrevPose set and the time rolled back, so velocity covers deformation as well as object and camera motion. Instanced meshes can opt into per-instance velocity.

Shading

Direct light is one analytic key: GGX distribution, height-correlated Smith visibility and Schlick Fresnel for specular, Lambert for diffuse, and a Charlie distribution with Neubelt visibility for sheen. The key's colour is irradiance, so diffuse is lightColor · n·l · albedo / π. Thin-sheet transmission lights the back face with a noise-free shadow lookup, so paper and fruit skin glow.

Indirect light is spherical-harmonics irradiance for diffuse plus a prefiltered environment for specular, using Karis' split-sum approximation with a multiple-scattering term so rough metals don't lose energy. Specular occlusion follows Lagarde. A day can add up to 16 analytic sphere occluders for soft contact darkening, exp(−strength · r²/d² · cos), faded out beyond a few radii.

The GGX specular lobe

D = α² / (π((n·h)²(α² − 1) + 1)²) with α = roughness², times the Smith visibility, plotted as reflected intensity over outgoing direction. Roughness is clamped to 0.03 so highlights never become a single pixel.

Shadows · ShadowPass.js · shaders/pipeline/shadow.js

Soft shadows that harden at contact

The shadow map is orthographic, 1024² on low, 2048² on medium and high, 4096² for capture, and stores depth in a float target plus a compare texture for hardware filtering. It is fitted to the shadow casters every frame (or to a sphere the day gives it). Its radius is quantised to eighth-octave steps and its centre snapped to the texel grid, so shadows don't shimmer as things move.

Lookups use PCSS. A blocker search of 24 taps finds the average depth of what is between the point and the light, and the penumbra grows with that gap: penumbra = gap · tan(light angle). Then 20 filtered taps soften the edge to that width. So a shadow is sharp where an object touches the floor and soft where it floats above it. Each pixel rotates its taps by white noise (rather than a pattern, which would show as hatching). A receiver-plane depth gradient, a slope-scaled normal offset and a fade at the terminator keep acne away.

With shadow denoise on, shadows are traced at half resolution instead and then accumulated over frames and blurred with the same filters as the indirect light below, edge-aware so contact shadows stay crisp; pixels too thin for the half-resolution buffer trace their own.

Penumbra from blocker distance

Light size is tan of the light's angular radius. The two discs on the right are the 24 blocker-search taps (radius linear in the index) and the 20 filtering taps (Vogel spiral).

The prepass · shaders/pipeline/octahedral.js

Normals, velocity and depth

Before shading, every opaque mesh is drawn once into a full-resolution RGBA16F target with float depth. Red and green hold the view-space normal, packed with the octahedral mapping. Blue and alpha hold the screen-space velocity between this frame and the last, with the frame's sub-pixel jitter removed. Everything after reads depth from here. The half-resolution buffers pack the world normal even tighter, as two 12-bit integers combined into one float32 (at most 2²⁴ − 1, which a float holds exactly), next to linear depth.

Octahedral normal mapping

Divide the normal by |x| + |y| + |z| so it lands on an octahedron; if it points away (z < 0), fold the lower half out to the square's corners: xy = (1 − |yx|)·sign(xy). Every direction gets a unique point in the square with nearly even precision.

Light bouncing · GIPass.js · shaders/pipeline/gi.js

Ambient occlusion and one bounce, from a bitmask

Ambient occlusion and indirect light come from one half-resolution pass: GTAO with a visibility bitmask. For each pixel the pass picks 1–3 slices (2 on high) around the view direction, rotated by noise. Along each slice it marches 8 steps outward on both sides (12 for capture), spaced quadratically so close detail gets more samples, out to one world unit or 64 half-res pixels.

Each sample is treated as a slab of a certain thickness, not an infinite wall. Its front and back edges map to angles, and the angles to a range of the hemisphere's 32 sectors, weighted by the cosine so the sectors carry equal irradiance. The sectors are bits in a 32-bit integer. Visibility is the share of bits left clear. And each sample lights the sectors it newly covers with last frame's lit colour at that spot, which is the bounce: colour bleeds from a red wall onto a white floor, a frame late.

One slice and its 32-sector mask

Thick slabs hide everything behind them; thin ones let the sky through behind, which is what stops thin railings from darkening whole walls. A sector's bit is set by the first sample that covers it; later samples behind it add no light.

Accumulating over time

One noisy frame of this is not usable, so each result is blended into a history buffer. The history is reprojected with the prepass velocity, and history samples are rejected where depth or normal disagree (a depth tolerance that loosens at grazing angles, and normals within about 37°). What survives is clamped to the mean ± 2.5 standard deviations of the current frame's 3 × 3 neighbourhood, so stale light can't linger. The blend is a running mean, 1 / min(frames + 1, 32), capped at 1/32 (1/48 on low).

Then 2 or 3 à-trous passes blur it: a 5 × 5 B3 kernel with doubling gaps, weighted by plane distance and normal agreement, so it smooths flat areas without bleeding over edges. In the main pass, each full-resolution pixel takes the four nearest half-resolution samples weighted by depth and normal match, falling back to the best of the 3 × 3 when none fit.

Clamping the history

Then out = mix(history, current, 1/n): after a clamp, the next frames pull the result toward the truth instead of keeping a ghost.

A day tunes how AO and GI land: ao = max(mix(1, visibility^aoPower, aoIntensity) · occlusion · spheres, aoMin). AO darkens the SH ambient (optionally tinted by aoColor), the GI adds gi · giIntensity to indirect diffuse, and aoDirect lets AO darken direct light too. Transparent materials get no AO or GI.

Motion blur · shaders/pipeline/motionBlur.js

A real shutter, even while paused

Motion blur is opt-in. It follows McGuire's 2012 reconstruction filter, measured over a shutter window in scene time, so a paused frame keeps its blur. A second prepass renders every mesh at its pose half a shutter ago: the day's own userData.shutterMatrixWorld if it sets one, otherwise one extrapolated from its last measured linear and angular velocity. The camera is not blurred.

Velocities are capped (3 % of the image height by default), reduced to the longest per 8 × 8 tile, then to the longest within reach of each tile. Each pixel then takes 24 samples (32 on capture) along the tile's dominant velocity and its own, alternating, with a Bayer jitter. Each sample is weighted by whether it is in front or behind (with a soft depth range) and whether its motion reaches the pixel: the classic cone and cylinder terms, so a moving edge smears over the background but the background never smears over it.

Tile max and neighbour max

Left: per-pixel velocity of a moving disc. Middle: the longest per 8 × 8 tile. Right: the longest within ceil(max blur / 8) tiles, which tells each pixel how far it must look for something that could blur over it.

Depth of field · DepthOfFieldPass.js

A fast gather, then the real lens

Depth of field is opt-in. Each pixel's circle of confusion is aperture · height · (1 − focus / z), signed so the near field is negative, and capped at 4 % of the height. A day sets the focus distance and the aperture, the blur of something infinitely far, every frame.

While the view moves, the pass gathers at half resolution. It averages each 2 × 2 block, records the maximum blur and the strongest foreground blur per 16-pixel tile, spreads those to neighbouring tiles so a blurred foreground can bleed over a sharp background, and takes 8 to 64 spiral samples, each counted if its own blur reaches the pixel.

When the camera, the scene, the time, the focus and the aperture all hold still, the pipeline switches to the real thing. It renders the scene again and again from points spread across the lens: the camera moves in its own plane by up to the aperture radius, and the projection shifts back so the focal plane stays put. A running mean of those frames gives exact blur, exact occlusion at every edge and antialiasing for free. The view crossfades from the gather to the mean over frames 8 to 32, and after 256 frames (Lens samples, up to 1024) it stops rendering and shows the stored average. Any change restarts it; a day calls restartAccumulation() after changing uniforms the pipeline can't see.

Blur circle and lens samples

Lens offsets are (√h₂(n)·cos 2πh₃(n), √h₂(n)·sin 2πh₃(n)) with Halton sequences in bases 2 and 3, so any prefix covers the disc evenly. Subpixel jitter uses bases 5 and 7. Aperture here is 0.02 of the height.

The finish · shaders/pipeline/final.js · bloom.js

Bloom, AgX and grain

A day can run its own full-screen pass over the lit HDR image through the composite hook, after motion blur and before depth of field and bloom; an earlier version of Day 2 ray-traced glass there.

Bloom builds a pyramid of up to 7 half-size levels with Jimenez's 13-tap downsample (with Karis averaging on the first level, so single hot pixels don't sparkle) and a tent upsample. It is mixed in as an energy-preserving lerp, mix(color, bloom, strength), not added, so it never brightens the image overall.

The final pass applies, in order: chromatic aberration (off by default), exposure, a vignette before tonemapping, the tonemapper (AgX by default; ACES, Khronos PBR Neutral or a plain clamp), sRGB encoding, saturation and contrast in display space, an optional clip warning, film grain that fades toward black and white, and a ±0.5/255 dither against banding. AgX converts to Rec.2020, applies an inset matrix, maps log₂ exposure over about 16.5 stops through a polynomial S-curve and back out, so highlights desaturate toward white smoothly instead of clipping to a hue.

Tonemappers on grey

Display value (sRGB-encoded) against scene value in stops around 0.18 grey. AgX keeps the longest highlight roll-off; the clip warning paints anything above 0.97.

The studio · studio.js

A studio baked into light

Most days light their scene with a procedural photo studio: a seamless curved cyc, a key softbox about 7 units away, a larger fill panel, an optional ceiling disc and a dim surround. It is baked once into a prefiltered environment map for reflections. The diffuse light comes from 9 spherical-harmonic coefficients projected from a second bake with the key hidden, because the key is also the analytic light that casts the shadows: its colour is the panel's power over 7², and the PCSS light size is 0.8 × tan of its angular size. So the key's reflection, its diffuse light and its soft shadow all come from the same panel without counting it twice. An HDR photo can be used instead, with the light direction taken from its SH.
